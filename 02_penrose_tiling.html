The Pentagrid Construction (de Bruijn 1981)
For each family $k \in \{0,1,2,3,4\}$, define parallel lines at angle $\theta_k = 2\pi k/5$:
$$G_k = \bigl\{\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^2 : \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{e}_k = n + \gamma_k,\; n\in\mathbb{Z}\bigr\}$$ where $\mathbf{e}_k = (\cos\theta_k, \sin\theta_k)$ and $\gamma_k \in [0,1)$ is an offset parameter.

Key constraint: $\sum_{k=0}^{4}\gamma_k = 0$ ensures the grid avoids triple intersections (generic position).

Each intersection of families $j \neq k$ maps to a rhombus tile. The index vector $(m_0, m_1, m_2, m_3, m_4) \in \mathbb{Z}^5$ with $m_k = \lceil \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{e}_k - \gamma_k \rceil$ projects two rhombus shapes: fat (72°) and thin (36°).
5D → 2D Projection
The tiling lives in a 2D physical space $E_\parallel$ embedded in $\mathbb{R}^5$. The projection vectors are:
$$\mathbf{v}^{(j)}_k = \cos(2\pi jk/5)\,\hat{e}_x + \sin(2\pi jk/5)\,\hat{e}_y \quad j=1,2$$ The perpendicular space $E_\perp \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ determines which lattice points project into the tiling window $W \subset E_\perp$. Changing $\gamma_k$ shifts the window, producing different (but locally indistinguishable) tilings.

Golden ratio in spacing: consecutive line spacings in family $k$ follow the ratio $\tau = \varphi = (1+\sqrt{5})/2$ — the same ratio as kite-to-dart tile counts.
Uncountably many tilings: Different $\gamma_k$ values give tilings that differ globally but are locally indistinguishable — any finite patch in one appears somewhere in every other. This is called local indistinguishability (Penrose 1979).

Quasicrystal Diffraction Theory
The structure factor for a set of atomic positions $\{\mathbf{r}_j\}$ is:
$$F(\mathbf{G}) = \sum_j f_j\, e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}_j}$$ For a Penrose quasicrystal, reciprocal lattice vectors require 5 integer indices:
$$\mathbf{G} = \sum_{k=0}^{4} h_k \mathbf{G}_k, \quad \mathbf{G}_k = \frac{2\pi}{a}\bigl(\cos(2\pi k/5),\,\sin(2\pi k/5)\bigr)$$ This gives a dense (not periodic) but discrete set of Bragg peaks — the hallmark of quasicrystals: sharp diffraction spots with icosahedral/pentagonal symmetry.
Physical Quasicrystals
● Al−Mn (Shechtman 1982) — first icosahedral quasicrystal
● Al−Cu−Fe — thermodynamically stable icosahedral phase
● Ho−Mg−Zn — perfect dodecahedral macroscopic shape
● Icosahedrite (Bindi 2009) — natural quasicrystal in Khatyrka meteorite

IUCr redefined “crystal” in 1992: any solid with an essentially discrete diffraction diagram — removing the periodicity requirement entirely.
Crystallographic restriction theorem: A periodic tiling in 2D can only have 2-, 3-, 4-, or 6-fold rotational symmetry. 5-fold is forbidden for periodic structures — which is precisely why Penrose tilings and quasicrystals are so remarkable.

Substitution Matrix & Eigenvalue
The inflation rules $T\to G+T$, $G\to T+G$ are encoded in the matrix:
$$M = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$$ Its eigenvalues satisfy $\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 1 = 0$, giving
$$\lambda_+ = \varphi^2 = \varphi+1 \approx 2.618, \quad \lambda_- = \varphi^{-2} \approx 0.382$$ After $n$ steps: tile counts $\mathbf{v}_n = M^n\mathbf{v}_0 \sim \lambda_+^n\,\mathbf{u}_+$ where $\mathbf{u}_+\propto(\varphi,1)$.

Self-similarity: scaling the tiling by $\varphi^2$ and subdividing recovers the exact same tiling — a fractal-like structure at every scale.
Inflation vs. Deflation
Inflate: scale each tile by $\varphi$, then split according to substitution rules → more, smaller tiles covering the same area.

Deflate: merge groups of tiles into larger tiles → fewer, bigger tiles.

The two operations are exact inverses. Starting from any valid tiling, infinite inflation or deflation always produces another valid Penrose tiling. This proves that every finite patch appears infinitely often.
Why φ² = φ + 1 matters: the area of a fat rhombus to a thin rhombus is exactly φ². After each inflation step, the total tile count multiplies by π² ≈ 2.618 — not 2, not 3, but precisely the golden ratio squared.

Matching Rules & Aperiodicity
Penrose tiles carry edge markings (arrows or indentations) such that tiles may only be assembled if markings on shared edges agree. There are two types of edge markings:
● Single arrow (S): must align head-to-tail
● Double arrow (D): must also align head-to-tail

Theorem (Penrose 1974): Any tiling of the plane using P2 tiles that satisfies all matching rules is necessarily aperiodic — no translation symmetry is possible.

Undecidability connection: Berger (1966) proved that the domino tiling problem is undecidable. Penrose’s matching rules show that local constraints can enforce global aperiodicity — a deep connection to computability theory.
Conway Worms & Ammann Bars
Conway worms (John H. Conway): infinite sequences of kites and darts forming straight strips. Any Penrose tiling contains exactly 5 families of Conway worms, parallel to the 5 pentagrid directions.

Ammann bars (Robert Ammann): line segments on each tile that, when tiles obey matching rules, extend into 5 families of infinite parallel lines — with a quasiperiodic spacing ratio of $\varphi:1$. These bars directly encode the pentagrid structure.
Local vs. global: matching rules are purely local (only adjacent tiles need agree) yet they enforce a global structure. This is philosophically profound — Penrose himself connected this to the question of whether the laws of physics (local) could enforce the global structure of spacetime.

λ-Adic Numbers & Almost Periodicity

Harald Bohr (1925) defined almost-periodic functions: $f(x)$ such that for any $\varepsilon>0$, the set of $\tau$ with $\sup_x|f(x+\tau)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ is relatively dense. Penrose tilings are almost-periodic in exactly this sense — they are the spatial analog of Bohr’s functions. The Fourier transform of an almost-periodic function is a discrete but dense set of frequencies, precisely matching quasicrystal diffraction.

Cut-and-Project & Model Sets

Yves Meyer (1972) introduced model sets (cut-and-project sets): take the integer lattice $\mathbb{Z}^5 \subset \mathbb{R}^5$, define a strip $\mathcal{S} = E_\parallel \times W$ where $W \subset E_\perp$ is a compact window (a regular pentagon for Penrose), and project $\mathcal{S} \cap \mathbb{Z}^5$ onto $E_\parallel$. The result is a Penrose tiling vertex set. Meyer’s theory proves these sets have pure point diffraction — the mathematical basis for sharp Bragg peaks in quasicrystals.

E₈ Root System & Icosahedral Quasicrystals

The $E_8$ root system has 240 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^8$. The icosahedral subgroup $Y \subset SO(3)$ of order 60 acts on a 6D sublattice of $E_8$. Projecting this onto 3D physical space plus 3D perpendicular space gives the 3D icosahedral quasicrystal. The same $E_8$ lattice appears in the heterotic string theory ($E_8 \times E_8$) and in Garrett Lisi’s $E_8$ theory of everything. The deepest tilings of physical space may be shadows of 8-dimensional geometry.

Kochen–Specker Contextuality

The Kochen–Specker theorem (1967) proves that quantum mechanics is contextual: measurement outcomes cannot be pre-assigned independently of which other observables are measured simultaneously. One construction uses a set of 31 rays in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with Penrose-like geometric structure. A Penrose tiling of the sphere gives a natural coloring problem whose unsolvability is the Kochen–Specker theorem — connecting aperiodic geometry directly to quantum foundations.

Noncommutative Geometry (Connes)

Alain Connes showed that the space of all Penrose tilings, while uncountable and topologically trivial (all tilings are locally indistinguishable), admits a rich noncommutative structure. The $C^*$-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ of the Penrose tiling has $K_0(\mathcal{A}) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ with the golden ratio $\varphi$ encoding the module structure. This is the first example of a physical application of noncommutative geometry — Connes used it as a motivating example in his 1994 book.

Penrose’s Quantum Mind Thesis

In The Emperor’s New Mind (1989) and Shadows of the Mind (1994), Penrose argues that human consciousness involves non-algorithmic processes, connected to his Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch-OR) hypothesis with Hameroff. The aperiodic structure of Penrose tilings mirrors his view: locally determined rules create globally non-computable structures. The undecidability of the tiling extension problem (can a finite patch extend to a full Penrose tiling?) connects directly to Gödel incompleteness.

“Road to Reality” connection: Penrose devotes Chapter 33 to quasicrystals and Chapter 1 to the golden ratio. The deeper point throughout the book is that the most profound mathematics — irrational numbers, infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, fiber bundles — turns out to be exactly what describes physical reality. Penrose tilings are a perfect microcosm of this theme.

Ammann-Beenker (1984): An octagonal (8-fold) quasicrystal tiling using squares and 45° rhombi. Discovered independently by Robert Ammann and F. P. M. Beenker.
Octagonal Quasicrystal Structure
The Ammann-Beenker tiling uses two prototiles: a square and a 45° rhombus (thin rhombus with acute angle 45°). The substitution rules are:
$$S \to 2S + 4R \qquad R \to 2S + 2R$$ where $S$ = square, $R$ = rhombus. The inflation factor is $1+\sqrt{2}$ (the silver ratio $\delta_S$).

8-fold symmetry: Unlike Penrose's 5-fold, this tiling has octagonal symmetry. The reciprocal lattice has 8-fold rotational symmetry, corresponding to quasicrystals with 8-fold diffraction patterns (e.g., Cr–Ni–Si alloys).
Silver Ratio $\delta_S = 1+\sqrt{2}$
The silver ratio satisfies $\delta_S^2 = 2\delta_S + 1$, analogous to $\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1$ for the golden ratio.
$$\delta_S = 1+\sqrt{2} \approx 2.4142$$ The tile area ratio (square : rhombus) = $\delta_S : 1$. After $n$ inflations, the tile count grows as $\delta_S^{2n}$.
Connection to Penrose: Both are examples of canonical projection tilings from 4D (Ammann-Beenker) and 5D (Penrose) lattices. The Ammann-Beenker tiling projects from the 4D hypercubic lattice $\mathbb{Z}^4$.

The Cut-and-Project Method (Meyer 1972, de Bruijn 1981)
Let $\mathbb{Z}^n \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be the integer lattice. Decompose $\mathbb{R}^n = E_\parallel \oplus E_\perp$ where $E_\parallel$ is physical space (dimension $d$) and $E_\perp$ is perpendicular space (dimension $n-d$).

Define the strip (or window) $W \subset E_\perp$ as a compact region. The tiling vertices are:
$$\mathcal{V} = \bigl\{\pi_\parallel(\mathbf{x}) : \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{Z}^n,\; \pi_\perp(\mathbf{x}) \in W\bigr\}$$ where $\pi_\parallel$ and $\pi_\perp$ are orthogonal projections onto $E_\parallel$ and $E_\perp$.

For Penrose tilings: $n=5$, $d=2$, $E_\parallel$ is the 2D plane spanned by the 5-fold symmetric vectors, and $W$ is a regular pentagon in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Model Sets & Pure Point Diffraction
Model sets (Meyer 1972) are cut-and-project sets where $W$ is compact and has non-empty interior. They have three fundamental properties:
● Pure point diffraction — the Fourier transform is a discrete set of Bragg peaks
● Meyer property — the set is relatively dense and uniformly discrete
● Finite local complexity — only finitely many local patterns up to translation

The window $W$ determines the acceptance domain: lattice points whose perpendicular coordinate falls inside $W$ are selected. Changing $W$ changes the tiling but preserves local indistinguishability.
Key insight: The irrational slope of $E_\parallel$ relative to the lattice ensures that the projected set is aperiodic. The compactness of $W$ ensures the set is uniformly discrete — no accumulation points.

Fibonacci word: The infinite word $ABAABABAABAAB\ldots$ generated by $A\to AB$, $B\to A$. Its letter ratio converges to $\varphi$.
Fibonacci Word & Substitution Sequences
The Fibonacci word is the fixed point of the substitution:
$$A \to AB,\qquad B \to A$$ Starting from $A_0 = A$, we get:
$A_1 = AB,\; A_2 = ABA,\; A_3 = ABAAB,\; A_4 = ABAABABA,\; \ldots$

The length $|A_n| = F_{n+2}$ (Fibonacci numbers). The ratio of $A$ to $B$ letters converges to $\varphi = (1+\sqrt{5})/2$.
1D Quasicrystal & Diffraction
Place atoms at positions $x_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \ell_i$ where $\ell_i \in \{L,S\}$ follows the Fibonacci word. Sharp Bragg peaks at $k_{m,n}=\frac{2\pi}{a}(m+n\varphi)$. Cut-and-project from $\mathbb{Z}^2$ with slope $1/\varphi$.
Sturmian words: exactly $n+1$ distinct subwords of length $n$ — the 1D analog of minimal complexity in aperiodic order.

Quasicrystal Diffraction Theory
The diffraction pattern of a quasicrystal is given by the Fourier transform of its density function $\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_j \delta(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_j)$:
$$\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{k}) = \sum_j e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}_j}$$ For a Penrose tiling, the reciprocal lattice vectors are integer combinations of $n$ basis vectors:
$$\mathbf{G} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} h_k \mathbf{e}_k, \quad \mathbf{e}_k = \bigl(\cos(2\pi k/n), \sin(2\pi k/n)\bigr)$$ The diffraction pattern is pure point (sharp Bragg peaks) but the peaks are dense in reciprocal space — they fill the plane but remain discrete.
Crystal vs. Quasicrystal Comparison
Crystal: Periodic lattice → reciprocal lattice is also periodic. Peaks indexed by $d$ integers. Only 2-, 3-, 4-, 6-fold symmetry allowed.

Quasicrystal: Aperiodic but ordered → reciprocal lattice is dense but discrete. Peaks indexed by $n > d$ integers. Any $n$-fold symmetry possible.

IUCr definition (1992): A crystal is any solid with an essentially discrete diffraction diagram. This redefinition explicitly includes quasicrystals.
Shechtman's discovery (1982): The Al–Mn alloy diffraction pattern showed sharp 10-fold symmetric spots — impossible for any periodic crystal. This earned the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Conway Worms & Local Isomorphism
A Conway worm is an infinite strip of Penrose tiles (kites and darts) that forms a straight chain. The tiles in a worm alternate in a specific sequence determined by the Fibonacci word.

Properties:
● Each worm is bounded by two parallel lines (the worm boundaries)
● The sequence of tiles along a worm is aperiodic but quasiperiodic
● Worms in different directions intersect at tile vertices
● The spacing between parallel worms follows a $\varphi:1$ ratio

Local isomorphism: Any finite patch of tiles in one Penrose tiling appears infinitely often in every other Penrose tiling. Conway worms are the mechanism: they allow patches to be transported along worm lines.
Ammann Bars & Grid Structure
Ammann bars are line segments drawn on each tile that, when matching rules are obeyed, align to form 5 families of infinite parallel lines. These bars are the direct geometric realization of the pentagrid construction.

The spacing between adjacent Ammann bars in family $k$ alternates between two values in the ratio $\varphi:1$, following the Fibonacci word. This is the 1D Fibonacci quasicrystal embedded in the 2D tiling.
Conway's discovery: John H. Conway showed that any Penrose tiling can be decomposed into worms. The worm structure provides a complete classification of Penrose tilings up to local isomorphism.

Danzer's 3D Aperiodic Tiling
Danzer's tiling uses four tetrahedral prototiles (A, B, C, D) that fill space aperiodically. The tiles have edge lengths in the golden ratio $\varphi$ and their dihedral angles are related to the icosahedron.

Substitution rules: Each tetrahedron is subdivided into smaller tetrahedra of the same four types, scaled by $\varphi$. The inflation factor is $\varphi^3$ (volume scaling).

The tiling has icosahedral symmetry — the same symmetry as the 3D Penrose tiling (icosahedral quasicrystal). It is the 3D analog of the 2D Penrose tiling.
Icosahedral Quasicrystals
The first icosahedral quasicrystal was discovered by Shechtman in Al–Mn (1982). Since then, many stable icosahedral phases have been found:
● Al–Cu–Fe — thermodynamically stable, perfect icosahedral symmetry
● Al–Pd–Mn — large single quasicrystals grown for structural studies
● Zn–Mg–Ho — forms perfect dodecahedral macroscopic crystals

The 3D Penrose tiling (icosahedral) is the geometric model for these materials. Danzer's tiling provides an alternative tetrahedral decomposition.
3D cut-and-project: The icosahedral tiling is obtained by projecting a 6D hypercubic lattice $\mathbb{Z}^6$ onto 3D physical space, with a 3D window (a triacontahedron) in perpendicular space.

$E_8$ & the Icosahedral Quasicrystal
The $E_8$ root lattice is an 8-dimensional lattice with 240 minimal vectors (roots). It is the largest exceptional Lie algebra and appears in:
● Heterotic string theory ($E_8 \times E_8$)
● Monster moonshine (the Monster group has 196,884 dimensions, related to $E_8$)
● Garrett Lisi's $E_8$ Theory of Everything

Elser-Sloane construction: Project $E_8$ onto a 3D subspace $E_\parallel$ that is invariant under the icosahedral group $Y \subset SO(3)$. The perpendicular space $E_\perp$ is 5D. The window $W \subset E_\perp$ is a 5D polytope whose projection gives the 3D quasicrystal.
4D → 3D Projection
A simpler construction uses the 4D hypercubic lattice $\mathbb{Z}^4$ projected to 3D. The 4D rotation group $SO(4)$ contains the icosahedral group as a subgroup. The projection of $\mathbb{Z}^4$ onto a 3D subspace with irrational slope gives a 3D aperiodic tiling with icosahedral symmetry.

The 600-cell (4D regular polytope with 120 vertices) projects to a 3D icosahedral cluster. The vertices of the 600-cell correspond to the 120 vertices of the 3D Penrose tiling's fundamental cell.
Deep connection: The $E_8$ lattice, the 600-cell, and the icosahedral quasicrystal are all manifestations of the same underlying structure: the golden ratio $\varphi$ and the binary icosahedral group.

Girih Tiles & Medieval Quasicrystals
Girih (Persian: گریه, "knot") is a decorative geometric art form used in Islamic architecture since the 15th century. The girih tile set consists of five shapes:
● Regular decagon (10 sides)
● Regular pentagon (5 sides)
● Regular hexagon (6 sides)
● Bowtie (concave hexagon)
● Rhombus (72°/108°)

These tiles have edge markings (like Penrose matching rules) that force aperiodic patterns. The Darb-e Imam shrine in Isfahan (1453 CE) contains a nearly perfect quasiperiodic girih pattern with 5-fold and 10-fold symmetry.
Lu & Steinhardt (2007) Discovery
In 2007, Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt published a landmark paper in Science showing that medieval Islamic architects had created quasiperiodic girih patterns using a set of five tiles with matching rules — essentially Penrose tilings, 500 years before Penrose.

The patterns at the Topkapi Scroll (Istanbul, ~1500 CE) show explicit girih tile layouts with inflation/deflation properties. The scroll contains instructions for constructing arbitrarily large quasiperiodic patterns using self-similar subdivision — the same inflation principle Penrose rediscovered in 1974.
Historical significance: This shows that the mathematical structure of aperiodic order was discovered independently by multiple cultures. The girih tiles are a genuine historical precursor to Penrose tilings.

Generalized inflation: In $d$ dimensions, the inflation factor is $\varphi^d$. The substitution matrix has eigenvalues $\varphi^d$ and $\varphi^{-d}$.
Generalized Substitution Rules
The Penrose substitution can be generalized to arbitrary dimension $d$ using the golden ratio $\varphi$ as the inflation factor. In $d$ dimensions:
$$T \to \varphi^d \cdot T$$ where $T$ is the tile volume. The substitution matrix $M_d$ has eigenvalues $\varphi^d$ and $\varphi^{-d}$.

2D (Penrose): $M_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $\varphi^2, \varphi^{-2}$
3D (icosahedral): $M_3$ is a $4\times4$ matrix with eigenvalues $\varphi^3, \varphi^{-3}$
4D (600-cell): $M_4$ has eigenvalues $\varphi^4, \varphi^{-4}$

The eigenvectors give the relative tile counts, which converge to ratios involving $\varphi$.
Pisot Numbers & Aperiodic Order
A Pisot number is a real algebraic integer $>1$ whose conjugates all have modulus $<1$. The golden ratio $\varphi$ is a Pisot number (the smallest, in fact).

Theorem (Meyer): A cut-and-project set has pure point diffraction if and only if the projection matrix has entries in a field generated by a Pisot number. This is why $\varphi$ (and $\delta_S = 1+\sqrt{2}$ for octagonal tilings) appear in quasicrystal theory.

The Pisot property ensures that the perpendicular-space coordinates of lattice points are uniformly distributed in the window, giving sharp Bragg peaks.
Open problem: Classify all possible inflation tilings in $d \ge 3$ dimensions. The full classification of aperiodic substitution tilings in 3D and higher remains an active research area.
